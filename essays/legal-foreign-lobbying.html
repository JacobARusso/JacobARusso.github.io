Legal Foreign Lobbying in the United States

April 3, 2026

Introduction

Growing up in an evangelical Christian household, I was surrounded by unwavering support for Israel - a position so embedded in my community's religious identity that I never questioned it. It wasn’t until I noticed the gap between the American public and congressional foreign policy that I began to question why elected officials seem to be more supportive of Israel than the American public. As an American citizen and future voter, I have a vested interest in ensuring my elected officials actually represent my interests, rather than the interests of foreign governments. This issue extends beyond myself, raising questions for all American voters about whether they are being fairly represented.

In the early 1960s, the United States was hesitant to support Israel to prevent Arab oil embargoes. However, after the Six Day War, the U.S. increasingly saw Israel as a strategic foothold in the Middle East to deter Soviet influence. This growing partnership laid the groundwork for the deeply entrenched strategic partnership the U.S. and Israel share - and to the controversies regarding Israeli influence in American politics.

A PAC (political action committee) is an organization that gathers campaign contributions from members and donates those funds to specific campaigns. AIPAC is the most prominent pro-Israel lobbying organization in the United States, advocating for a strong alliance between the U.S. and Israel.

In recent years, especially in the aftermath of the October 7th attacks on Israel by Hamas, the United States’ relationship with Israel has been under increasing scrutiny. In 2025, a poll by Laura Silver, an expert in international survey research at Pew Research Center, found that the general population's support for Israel plummeted, dropping 11% in three years, yet bipartisan-supported bills continue to pass in support of sending economic and military support to Israel (Silver). This difference highlights an incongruence between the interests of the U.S. population and the foreign policy decisions of elected officials.

The foundational principle of a representative democracy is for elected officials to represent their constituents. Citizens, in deciding who to vote for, consider how they will be benefited from their choice. It is imperative that elected officials clearly represent the interests of their voters, beyond the influence of foreign entities, to make sure all American citizens are fairly represented. If evidence suggests that foreign entities, such as Israel, may exert disproportionate influence on American politics, then it raises the need for Americans to consider limiting that influence to preserve the foundations of American representative democracy.

This paper examines the extent of Israeli influence in contemporary American politics and whether that influence should be reduced. The gap between American public opinion and congressional foreign policy decisions raises questions about whether the state of Israel has disproportionate influence in American politics, and if that influence undermines the principles of American representative democracy.

Literature Review

The Israel Lobby

While pro-Israel PACs, like AIPAC, operate independently from the state of Israel under American campaign finance law, researchers and watchdog groups have documented a consistent pattern of pro-Israeli PAC campaign contributions aligning with congressional voting behavior and with the state of Israel’s political agenda, though some scholars contest whether this pattern demonstrates meaningful foreign influence. John J. Mearsheimer, a professor of political science at the University of Chicago, and Stephen M. Walt, a professor of international affairs at Harvard University, argue that AIPAC functions to keep U.S. policy aligned with Israel rather than broader American interests. They highlight how pro-Israel groups use campaign contributions to encourage “politicians to adopt pro-Israel positions” (Mearsheimer et al. 3). This suggests that AIPAC campaign contributions are deliberately directed towards legislative outcomes that support Israel’s foreign policy agenda, reinforcing the pattern scholars have identified between AIPAC financing and pro-Israel legislative outcomes. Corroborating this assessment, Track AIPAC, a watchdog organization that monitors congressional campaign contributions, documents a recurring pattern between pro-Israel campaign funding and pro-Israel legislative outcomes (Track AIPAC). This observational data reinforces the scholarly argument that pro-Israel campaign contributions are associated with pro-Israel foreign policy decisions. Not all scholars accept this characterization, however. Dennis Ross, a former U.S. diplomat and Middle East peace negotiator, disagrees with Mearsheimer and Walt’s characterization of pro-Israel lobbying and argues that lobbying does not distort U.S. foreign policy decisions (Mearsheimer et al. 6). Instead, Ross argues that the correlation between campaign financing and pro-Israel legislative outcomes is an oversimplification of policy outcomes, which are influenced by a multitude of factors. This disagreement among scholars highlights the central tension in the literature: While the pattern between pro-Israeli PAC campaign contributions and legislative outcomes is well documented, whether that pattern constitutes undue foreign influence is deeply contested.

Foreign Policy Alignment

The literature demonstrates that Israel and the U.S. consistently align on foreign policy decisions, though scholars remain deeply divided over whether this alignment is indicative of disproportionate influence from pro-Israel lobbying organizations or shared values and interests. The U.S. State Department, responsible for advising U.S. foreign policy and managing international relations, found in a 2023 report to Congress that the U.S. and Israel voted together on issues 94% of the time, higher than any other United Nations member state (United States Department of State 58). This near-total voting convergence underlies the central scholarly debate on the reason for Israeli-U.S. voting alignment. On one hand, Eytan Gilboa, professor of International Communications at Bar-Ilan University and scholar of U.S.-Israel relations, contends that the alignment between Israel and the U.S. is because of a combination of strategic interests and democratic values (Gilboa 473). During the Cold War, the United States and Israel created a strategic alliance to prevent communism from spreading to the Middle East. In the post-Cold War era, the United States and Israel cooperate to combat radical Islam and terrorist sects, like Hezbollah. Gilboa further identifies shared commitments to “liberal democracy” and “Judeo-Christian” cultural heritage as reasons for Israeli-U.S. alignment (Gilboa 474). Together these arguments suggest that the 94% voting alignment represents shared ideological and strategic values rather than the product of lobbying pressure. On the other hand, Jon Hoffman, a Ph.D in political science and policy analyst at the Cato Institute, contends that U.S.-Israel partnership costs the United States billions and worsens U.S. regional relations for comparatively little in return (Hoffman). Hoffman’s argument that an Israeli-U.S. partnership produces disproportionate costs with minimal strategic return undermines the shared values justification for a 94% vote alignment, lending weight to the argument that pro-Israel lobbying organizations sustain the strategic relationship between the U.S. and Israel beyond American strategic interests. Ultimately, the debate between U.S.-Israel foreign policy alignment reveals a fundamental tension in American foreign policy: whether the relationship reflects a genuine strategic and ideological partnership, or whether pro-Israel lobbying organizations sustain an alignment that extends beyond American foreign policy interests.

Public Opinion vs. Congressional Policy

Scholars and polling organizations have documented a growing divide between American public opinion and congressional foreign policy, though scholars remain divided whether this gap demonstrates disproportionate pro-Israel lobbying or broader shifts in America’s demographics. John J. Mearsheimer, a professor of political science at the University of Chicago, and Stephen M. Walt, a professor of international affairs at Harvard University, argue that pro-Israel lobbying campaign contributions are responsible for bipartisan support of a U.S.-Israel partnership (Mearsheimer et al. 6). This suggests a gap between voter opinions and Congressional outcomes is due to pro-Israel lobbying. Meanwhile, Laura Silver, an expert in international survey research at the Pew Research Center, documents that adults over fifty tend to have the most support for Israel (Silver). Eytan Gilboa, a professor of International Communications at Bar-Ilan University, interprets this demographic divide as evidence of institutional lag rather than lobbying influence, contending that congressional legislative outcomes more accurately represent the older generation whose consensus has not yet changed (Gilboa 473). This scholarly divide reveals that the existence of a gap between legislative foreign policy outcomes and voter opinions is real, whether that gap reflects the financial influence of pro-Israel lobbying organizations or a lag in Congressional representation is still contested.

Argument

The question of whether Israel has undue influence in American politics evokes deep emotions from both sides. Support for or skepticism of Israel is one of a few issues in American politics that transcends traditional bipartisan lines. For many Americans, specifically Evangelical Christians and members of the Jewish community, the partnership between the United States and Israel signifies a sacred alliance, rooted in spiritual, ideological, and strategic values. For others, the persistent congressional support for Israel despite a shifting voter opinion raises serious questions about whether American foreign policy reflects the will of the voters, a discussion that challenges the foundation of America’s representative democracy.

Regardless of whether one supports a U.S.-Israeli strategic partnership or not, every American has a shared interest in protecting the core principles of the American political system. The question about whether Israel has undue influence in American politics has nothing to do with Israel itself. Instead, it is about preserving the most core characteristics of voter representation. People who fervently support or oppose a U.S.-Israeli partnership can both agree that American foreign policy should reflect the will of the voters, not of a foreign entity. By discussing whether Israel has undue influence in American politics, we are not attacking the idea of a U.S.-Israeli partnership, but attempting to preserve voter representation.

I will make the case that Israel has disproportionately more influence in American politics than it should, and America must work to decrease that influence to preserve the foundation of American representative democracy. To reduce the undue influence of Israel in American politics, the power of lobbying organizations that push the agenda of foreign states, like Israel, must be curtailed by preventing them from choosing which candidate receives funds from their campaign contributions.

Lobbying has long been a point of contention in American politics. Detractors of lobbying argue that it allows wealthy special interest groups to override voter outcomes while supporters of lobbying argue that it is impossible to run a state or national campaign without large scale donations from lobbying groups. The ethics of lobbying is too large a topic to address in this paper; however, a critical distinction exists between domestic and foreign lobbying that permeates this paper. Even if one grants that domestic lobbying skews voter representation, domestic lobbying at least benefits a segment of the American public. For example, when a teachers union lobbying group influences legislation, whether one agrees with it or not, the policy still benefits a portion of the American population. Foreign lobbying is different because it never directly benefits the American citizen - it may only ever benefit them indirectly. When AIPAC, in accordance with the state of Israel’s perspective, aggressively lobbied Trump to end the Iranian Nuclear Deal in 2018 (JCPOA) established under Obama in 2015, a strong preventive measure against Iran developing nuclear weapons was ended in return for no substantive benefit (Field). In fact, one of the requirements the United States has in a peace deal with Iran is the prevention of Iranian nuclear weapon creation (Shamin). The Iranian nuclear deal illustrates how foreign lobbying outcomes serve Israeli security preferences while costing the American public. The United States now faces a more nuclearized Iran, ballooning oil prices, and regional instability, yet Israel got exactly what they wanted.

An important distinction must be made in my argument, I do not believe that the state of Israel has direct influence in lobbying outcomes in the United States, that is conspiratorial and not quantifiable. Instead, the clear policy alignment between American pro-Israel lobbying organizations and the agenda of the State of Israel allows Israel to further its agenda in American politics through the means of proxies. The overwhelming policy similarities between pro-Israel lobbying organizations and the state of Israel creates a system in which, functionally, the state of Israel has influence in American politics, albeit without direct intervention. This is not conspiratorial because the state of Israel does not have any direct involvement in this process. This is simply the predictable outcome of a system that allows PACs with foreign loyalties to contribute large sums of money to certain campaigns. Also, the reason I single out pro-Israel lobbying groups is because they are the largest and most politically relevant foreign lobbying group in contemporary politics, one could switch out Israel with Iran, Russia, Germany, etc. and I would still oppose their lobbying efforts.

Eytan Gilboa makes a strong argument for shared interests between the United States and Israel in “US-Israel Relations at 75.” He argues that shared beliefs in liberal democracy, religious values, and strategic interests are the primary reason why the United States and Israel converge in most policy decisions (474). There is no denying that the United States and Israel share some strategic similarities, both countries want to prevent a nuclear Iran and spread democracy in the Middle East. However, shared strategic interests alone cannot account for the 94% voting alignment between the United States and Israel in the UN in 2023 (United States Department of State 58). If alignment was purely strategic and ideological, the United States would not oftentimes stand alone while its closest allies - like the UK, Germany, and France - who also share similar democratic values yet tend to vote differently from Israel far more frequently, suggesting that financial lobbying is the primary incentive in the high degree of U.S.-Israeli foreign policy alignment.

Proponents and detractors of a U.S.-Israel strategic partnership can both agree that foreign intervention in American politics decreases voter representation. My argument does not call for the cessation of a strategic partnership between the two countries. I only want to reduce the influence of pro-Israel lobbying groups because of their functional alignment with the state of Israel, which create a system where American legislators are financially incentivized to represent foreign interests instead of the interests of their own constituents.

Conversations about the role of pro-Israel lobbying groups are imperative to protect our democracy. By having conversations about the pro-Israel lobby, Americans can find common ground on an issue that is incredibly divisive and work towards a solution that creates a lobbying structure that accurately represents the will of the people.

Conclusion

I have proposed a practical and realistic solution to the issue of pro-Israel lobbying decreasing voter representation. By preventing pro-Israel PACs from choosing which candidate they donate to, pro-Israel PACs will no longer be able to strategically contribute to specific campaigns to gain Congressional votes. Instead, these pro-Israel PACs would contribute to overall party funds, which removes the direct financial incentive for legislators to adopt pro-Israel positions (Track AIPAC). Without the direct financial incentive to support Israel, legislators can more accurately represent their voter base and still receive funding from their party fund. A reformed campaign finance system that restricts candidate-specific campaign contributions would preserve the U.S.-Israeli strategic partnership while increasing American voter representation.

American voters must demand that their representatives prioritize constituent interests over the interests of foreign entities by reforming campaign finance laws with my proposed solution. For evangelical families that see Israel as sacred, for Jewish communities who want to build a strong partnership with Israel, and for skeptics of the U.S.-Israeli partnership - this reform is not about Israel. It is about our representation.
